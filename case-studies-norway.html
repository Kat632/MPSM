What happens when a kindergarten moves into a museum?

In Bergen, Norway, two kindergartens spent eight days each at the University Museum of Bergen, turning the museum into a place for everyday life, play, discovery and learning.

My Kindergarten Is at the Museum

Developed during the 2018–2019 school year, this pilot project brought together two kindergartens, the University Museum of Bergen and Western Norway University of Applied Sciences. Rather than visiting for an hour or two, the children made the museum their temporary base, exploring its collections, rooms and surrounding green spaces as part of their everyday lives.

Bergen · University Museum of Bergen

A kindergarten in residence

Two groups of 16 pre-school children each spent eight days at the museum over a two-week period. Each group had its own permanent base, with time to settle into new routines and build relationships with museum staff.

The museum became more than somewhere to visit. It became a place where the children could play, explore, develop familiarity and begin to feel at home.

What difference did it make?

Spending time in the museum helped children develop a sense of belonging, follow their curiosity and form relationships with the people and places around them.

Belonging

Having their own base and returning to familiar spaces helped the children develop a strong sense of ownership of the museum.

Curiosity

Exhibitions, historical objects and the natural world prompted questions, discoveries and conversations that continued beyond individual activities.

Play

Imaginative stories, drawing, drama and free play allowed the children to make their own meanings from the museum and its collections.

Connection

Children built relationships with museum staff and later returned with family members to show them the places they had come to know.

When asked whether they had been playing in the museum, the children all said yes. Asked when, their answer was: “All the time”.

Rios and Ødemotland, 2019

The children's responses suggested that play had become part of their everyday experience in the museum. They explored its rooms, engaged with the collections and developed attachments to particular places and stories.

Some children later brought their families to the museum and showed them around. One child even asked a museum host to unlock the Crypt, the room that had served as his kindergarten group's base. These experiences suggested that the museum had become somewhere familiar and meaningful to the children.

Bergen · Norway · 2018–2019

A kindergarten in residence

Moving everyday life into the museum

My Kindergarten Is at the Museum was a pilot project developed in collaboration with Western Norway University of Applied Sciences, the University Museum of Bergen and audience development staff at Bergen Municipality's Department for Climate, Culture and Industry.

Inspired by My Primary School Is at the Museum, the project explored what might happen if a kindergarten temporarily replaced its familiar surroundings with a museum. The intention was to move beyond short visits and give children time to develop familiarity, identity and a sense of ownership of a cultural institution.

Two groups, eight days each

During the 2018–2019 school year, one kindergarten took part in the autumn and another in the spring. Each group included 16 pre-school children and spent eight days at the museum over two weeks.

The autumn group used the Crypt as its permanent base, while the spring group worked from the Studio. These spaces gave the children somewhere familiar to return to during their time in the museum.

Preparation began in the kindergartens. Children discussed what a museum might be like, considered what they might encounter there, drew pictures of their forthcoming experience and packed suitcases for the move.

Making the transition an adventure

The suitcases helped turn the move into a memorable event. The children travelled to the museum with their bags, which stayed there throughout the project. At the end of the experience, they packed up and returned to their kindergartens.

Back at the kindergarten, the suitcases could provide a starting point for recalling experiences, drawing, storytelling and creating displays that connected the museum with everyday life at the kindergarten.

A familiar companion called Laura

The museum was a large and sometimes challenging environment for a group of young children. To help bring the group together, the museum introduced Laura, a giant green caterpillar made with a hemp-rope structure that the children could hold.

Laura first appeared as a mysterious character in the museum's invitation. The children were intrigued by the idea of waking her from a coffin. Once they met her, she became a much-loved companion who accompanied them through the museum and helped them move around together.

Building relationships with museum staff

Museum staff led activities and introduced the children to the collections. The programme included explorations of Viking history, ancient Egypt, church art, natural history and the museum's botanical garden.

The kindergarten staff also needed practical arrangements that supported everyday life, including a permanent base, places to eat, washing facilities and access to toilets. The experience showed the importance of combining the museum's subject knowledge with the kindergarten staff's understanding of children's needs.

Play, exploration and cross-curricular learning

Discovering the museum together

Stories that brought history to life

The children encountered Viking history, archaeological discoveries, church art and ancient Egypt. One particularly memorable story concerned the theft of objects from the Viking exhibition. The mystery caught the children's imagination, and one group searched the exhibition for objects that had been returned.

The children talked about the story and represented it through drawings. Their responses included the thieves, the stolen treasure and the people who helped put things right again.

Exploring the natural world

The plastic whale prompted discussion about plastic and marine animals. The children also explored the botanical garden, where they could see, smell and touch plants, discover unfamiliar species and learn about caring for the garden.

The programme connected the museum's collections with children's experiences of the natural world, encouraging them to observe, ask questions and make their own connections.

Play as part of everyday museum life

Time in the museum included facilitated activities, drawing, drama, observation and opportunities for self-directed play. The children also visited nearby Nygårdsparken, where open lawns, trees and play opportunities offered a different kind of environment.

The children's own accounts suggested that play was woven throughout their museum experience, rather than being confined to a separate activity.

From visitors to a sense of ownership

Feedback from both kindergartens was positive. Children and adults described developing a strong sense of ownership of the museum and its surroundings.

Some children returned with their families and showed them around. Kindergarten staff also reported a heightened interest in museums, with one describing how her family had visited all five museums during the two-week period.

The project suggested that sustained time in a museum can create opportunities for children to build relationships with its spaces, collections and people. The researchers noted that further data analysis, interviews and conversations were still planned.
