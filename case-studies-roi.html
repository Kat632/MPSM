My Primary School is at the National Gallery of Ireland

In February, two classes from Catherine McAuley National School became the first school in residence at the National Gallery of Ireland. Across two weeks, the gallery became a place to investigate ideas, make connections and learn across the curriculum.

What difference did it make?

The residency gave children time, space and responsibility to shape their learning and share it with a wider audience.

Confidence

Children shared their learning with visitors and led tours for pupils from their own school.

Curiosity

Paintings, objects, archives and the gallery environment prompted questions and investigations that crossed subject boundaries.

Ownership

Children helped direct their learning and curated an exhibition that presented their work to the public.

Connection

Gallery staff, the school community and families became part of the experience, with the exhibition welcoming more than 2,000 visitors.

Learning was shaped by the children’s interests and by the possibilities of being in the gallery, rather than by a single subject or classroom lesson.

Summary of the project reflection

The residency connected the curriculum with the real place, its collections and the people who care for them. The teacher’s reflection emphasised that the gallery’s atmosphere and the children’s sense of ownership were central to the experience, and that this kind of learning could not simply be recreated in a classroom with a printed image.

Catherine McAuley National School · Dublin

A school in residence

Making the gallery their own

In February, two classes from Catherine McAuley National School spent two weeks at the National Gallery of Ireland, becoming the first school to take part in a school-in-residence programme there. Gallery staff, curators and art handlers helped the children explore how a gallery works and how its collection can open up different ways of learning.

The children visited the gallery’s archives and library and encountered objects and stories that extended their understanding of art and history. One particularly memorable experience was holding Jack B. Yeats’ 1924 Olympic medal.

Children's ideas lead the way

The residency encouraged pupils to follow their questions and make connections between what they saw, what they already knew and what they wanted to find out. Their investigations included urban living and pigeons, sensory exploration, geography and mapping, as well as creative responses to the gallery and its collection.

With artist Fergus Byrne, the children took part in a workshop and developed a collaborative artwork. They also explored ideas through drama, including tableau and direct speech, and made links with history and mathematics through perimeter, area, patterns and time.

Sharing their work

Pupils curated their own exhibition, which was displayed over two Open Studio weekends and seen by more than 2,000 visitors. They also led tours for children from their own school, sharing what they had discovered and made during the residency.

The project supported confidence and a sense of ownership and belonging. It also created opportunities for families to engage with the children's learning. One parent reported that their child had begun considering art as a subject to pursue at secondary school.

Cross-curricular learning

From the collection to the curriculum

Many subjects, shared experiences

The gallery provided a setting for learning across subjects rather than treating each one in isolation. Children explored geography through mapping and considered urban living through the subject of pigeons. Drama activities included tableau and direct speech, while mathematics emerged through perimeter, area, patterns and time.

Creative work included making origami currachs and developing personal responses to the song and idea of “Little Green Fields”. Pupils kept daily diary entries to record their experiences and thinking.

Working with artists and gallery staff

Gallery staff, curators and art handlers introduced pupils to different aspects of the institution. The children also explored the archives and library and worked with artist Fergus Byrne on a workshop and a collaborative artwork.

Learning that belongs to the children

The teacher reflected that the children helped direct their own learning. The gallery's spaces and atmosphere, and the children's feeling that the place belonged to them too, were important parts of the experience. The final exhibition gave pupils a real audience for their work and a chance to take responsibility for presenting it.
