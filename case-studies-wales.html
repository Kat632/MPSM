What happens when a primary school takes its learning to the waterfront?

At the National Waterfront Museum in Swansea, reception children from St Thomas Community Primary School spent two five-week periods learning through museum collections, interactive activities and the stories of Welsh industry and innovation.

My Primary School is at the National Waterfront Museum

In 2016, two reception classes from St Thomas Community Primary School became part of the National Waterfront Museum in Swansea. The project gave four and five-year-olds the opportunity to learn through objects, stories, workshops and experiences beyond the classroom.

Swansea · National Waterfront Museum

A school at the museum

Across two five-week projects, children explored the museum with their teachers and learning staff. Activities included marine life, toys, pirate adventures and the story of an early Welsh aircraft.

Reading, writing and numeracy continued alongside practical experiences, with opportunities to learn about their heritage and explore the world around them.

Inside the Swansea Project

Two reception classes at the museum

In 2016, St Thomas Community Primary School partnered with the National Waterfront Museum in Swansea to provide two projects for its youngest pupils. The first ran from 22 February to 24 March, involving 27 children aged four and five. The second ran from 16 May to 24 June, involving a further 24 children of the same age.

Class teachers Laura Luxton and Claire Stallwood planned learning around the museum's activities and workshops, working closely with learning officer Leisa Bryant. The museum's collections, spaces and educational programme became resources for learning across the curriculum.

Learning through objects and experiences

Children met marine biologists from Swansea University who brought fish, snails and crabs from their trawl. Handling the creatures encouraged children to observe their features, ask questions and develop their vocabulary.

A workshop about toys invited parents to learn alongside their children. On Pirate Day, children and teachers dressed up and worked with an actor to explore boats, the marina and life at sea.

The museum sessions also included reading, writing and numeracy activities that would normally take place in school. The class visited the leisure centre opposite the museum for PE lessons, and the local art gallery hosted a day of creative activities.

The story of the Robin Goch

One particularly memorable experience centred on the Robin Goch monoplane, built by Charles Horace Watkins and displayed hanging from the ceiling in one of the museum's atria.

A costumed actor playing Watkins told the story of Icarus and introduced the children to flight and the basic principles of aircraft design. By involving the children as participants in the story, the session captured their imaginations. The Robin Goch became a favourite theme throughout the project.

Learning beyond the classroom

The project gave teachers the chance to explore the museum's artefacts in greater depth and experiment with sensory, participative approaches to teaching. The museum gained a better understanding of how younger children engage with its collections and activities.

The experience also demonstrated the value of partnerships between schools, museums and local communities, while creating opportunities to reach families who had not previously visited the museum.

Learning and Impact

What Difference Did It Make?

Confidence and independence

Teachers reported that children became more confident and independent during the project. They developed personal skills involving toileting, organisation and preparation, as well as managing separation from their parents at the start of the day.

Language and communication

The museum's displays, objects, workshops and stories gave children opportunities to encounter new words, talk about what they could see and share their ideas. Their teachers observed children who had previously spoken very little becoming more animated, vocal and willing to communicate with their peers.

Relationships and social development

The experience helped children bond with one another and develop their social skills and manners. Learning together in a different environment created shared experiences and opportunities to interact in new ways.

Benefits for the school

Teachers gained confidence in delivering the curriculum outside the classroom and had opportunities to explore museum artefacts in greater depth. They also benefited from the museum's approach to sensory learning and the use of real objects in teaching.

Benefits for the museum

The project helped the National Waterfront Museum understand how younger children engage with its collections and activities. It encouraged staff to experiment with more participative sessions, develop their practice and strengthen relationships with local schools and families.

The long-term impact of this project will be for us to develop deeper engagement opportunities with local schools.

Nia Williams, Head of Learning, Participation and Interpretation

Challenges and lessons learned

Daily travel between the school and museum was tiring for the children. Staff also had to adapt to working without their usual classroom resources and the familiar environment of the school building. Despite these challenges, the head teacher reported that the project had operated surprisingly smoothly.
