What happens when schools and museums work together to rethink where, how and what children learn?

What difference does it make?

The Class in Residence project showed that moving learning into a cultural setting can affect far more than what children know.

Confidence

Children became more confident talking about what they had learned and sharing their experiences with others.

Curiosity

Real objects, places and experiences gave children reasons to ask questions and investigate the world around them.

Better learning

Speaking, writing, mathematics, science and creative work could be connected through one shared experience.

Connection

Teachers, museum staff, parents and children became part of the same learning community.

“When we came back on Friday both my teaching assistant and I said ‘Gosh, we’re exhausted. Wasn’t it so worthwhile? It was really really worthwhile.’”

Lingfield Primary teacher

The project was intensive for the adults as well as the children. But that intensity became part of the value of the experience, giving the teacher and museum education officer an opportunity to plan, teach and reflect together.

Lingfield Primary School · Marton, Middlesbrough

Class in Residence

The idea

Could some of the benefits of an extended museum-school relationship be achieved in a single week?

Inspired by the My Primary School is at the Museum model, a Year 1 class from Lingfield Primary relocated its learning from school to Captain Cook Birthplace Museum and Stewart Park for five days.

The aim was not simply to take lessons out of the classroom. The teacher and museum education officer planned the curriculum together, using the museum, its collections and the park as the learning environment.

At a glance

School
Lingfield Primary
Year group
Year 1
Children
31
Theme
Dinosaur Planet

What they did

The dinosaur theme became a way into a surprisingly broad range of learning.

  • Handled fossils and bones.
  • Dressed up and role-played palaeontologists.
  • Took part in a dinosaur hunt in Stewart Park.
  • Worked with an artist to design and make a felt dinosaur.
  • Used Google Expeditions to explore dinosaurs.
  • Created dinosaur fact files.
  • Explored the museum galleries and learned about Captain Cook.
  • Learned about continents and geography.
  • Discovered the work of local Victorian geologist Lewis Hunton.
  • Practised phonics and mathematical positional language through games and conversation.

Maths, English, History, Geography, Art and Design, Computing and PHSE were all woven through the week.

What changed?

The impact went well beyond the knowledge the children gained about dinosaurs.

Knowledge and skills

Children developed knowledge around dinosaurs, palaeontology, geology, archaeology and geography, alongside skills in coding, historical enquiry, creative and critical thinking, independent learning, speaking and listening.

Language and writing

Vocabulary grew throughout the week, including mathematical positional language. The children also showed improvements in spelling, handwriting and motivation to write.

Confidence and behaviour

Children were highly motivated to attend and learn. The teacher reported no distracting behavioural incidents during the week.

Ownership

The museum and park became places the children felt belonged to them. They developed a genuine sense of ownership over the spaces where they were learning.

“The dinosaur is between the rock and the flower and he is very big and likes eating grass.” A pupil's written work, observed during the project

They couldn't stop talking about it

The experience was noticeable at home as well as at school. Parents reported that children were more confident talking about their days and were unusually enthusiastic about going to school.

“He has enjoyed the experience. He seems more confident to discuss what he did during the day and who he did it with.” Parent
“He was very excited during the week, and enthusiastic to learn. He was worried he'd miss the school bus on a morning so actually got dressed no problem.” Parent

The impact went beyond the museum

Almost all of the children's families attended the final celebration afternoon. The children presented their work and showed their parents what they had learned.

The experience was so successful that the school decided to introduce similar sharing afternoons for other year groups. Within a month, two other year groups had held their own sharing afternoons.

Learning worked both ways

The project was valuable professional development for the teacher too. Planning and delivering lessons in a completely different environment encouraged reflection on classroom practice and opened up new possibilities for using hands-on resources.

“As a class teacher, it was great professional development to work with the education officer from the museum to plan, deliver and evaluate lessons.” Lingfield Primary teacher

The museum learned too

The education officer gained a better understanding of classroom practice and the demands placed on teachers each day. The partnership also highlighted useful approaches and activities that could inform the museum's wider learning programme.

Most importantly, the experience helped establish an ongoing relationship between the school and the museum.

What made it work?

  • Close planning between the teacher and museum education officer.
  • Support from the teaching assistant, parents, park and museum staff.
  • A topic that connected naturally with the museum's collections.
  • A creative and playful approach to learning.
  • Using different museum and park spaces throughout the week.
  • Involving parents and carers in the final afternoon.
“Gosh, we're exhausted. Wasn't it so worthwhile? It was really really worthwhile.” Lingfield Primary teacher

Liverpool · 2016

My Primary School is at Tate Liverpool

Building on an established relationship, Kensington Children's Centre and nursery based a group of pre-school children at Tate Liverpool for two weeks.

Both Tate and nursery staff were surprised at how quickly the children felt relaxed in their new surroundings and how well they behaved in the public spaces. The children began talking more, experimenting with their play and developing greater independence.

The setting

Kensington Children's Centre delivers childcare for children aged six months to five years, with learning through play and challenging activities. The nursery already had an established relationship with cultural organisations including Tate Liverpool.

Tate Liverpool is housed in a converted warehouse at Liverpool's Albert Dock and displays work from the national collection of British art, alongside international modern and contemporary art.

Two weeks in the gallery

The project ran from 29 February to 11 March 2016. Twenty-four pre-school children aged three to four took part, with a maximum of fifteen children in the gallery each day.

Tate Liverpool's usual Early Years programme lasted around two hours. Having the children at the gallery for most of the day created a much more relaxed and open-ended dynamic between the nursery practitioners and Tate staff.

Based in the Clore Learning Centre, the children were encouraged to explore the gallery's resources and move between spaces according to their interests.

“We wanted them to have as much fun as possible and enjoy it, but feel safe and be comfortable and feel like they own these spaces.” Debbie Goldsmith, Tate Liverpool

Following the children's interests

A specialist arts practitioner created a large visual 'discovery tree' on the wall. This mapped the children's interests and linked them to works in the collection and resources brought in from the nursery, including toys, dolls and paintings.

Activities were added to the tree throughout the project, creating a visual record of how individual children's interests developed over the two weeks.

The children could choose from a wide range of materials including clay, sand, paints, string and straws, alongside wooden blocks, toy animals, dolls and tea sets.

“They actually wrapped the blocks into the tin foil and stood them all up and said it just looks like the art.” Marie Harper, Kensington Children's Centre

Beyond the gallery

The project also included trips beyond Tate Liverpool. Tate staff accompanied the children to the Museum of Liverpool and on a ferry trip, extending the children's experience of the waterfront and the wider cultural environment.

At the gallery's Art Gym, the children helped prepare for a party by making lanterns for the LED lights. They also enjoyed dancing and mixing with other gallery visitors.

“This project was much more exploratory and child-led. What they were interested in wasn't always what I would have picked out.” Katy McCall, Tate Liverpool

What changed?

For the children

  • New friendships developed.
  • Children became more confident speaking, particularly in group discussions.
  • Children demonstrated greater independence, including choosing their own meals at the gallery café.
  • The variety of materials and activities allowed children's imaginations to flourish.
“We did have a wow-moment today. One of the children spoke for the first time to another child.” Marie Harper, Kensington Children's Centre

For the nursery

  • Practitioners were able to test ideas in a new environment with the support of experienced facilitators.
  • Ideas and approaches from the gallery could be transferred back into the nursery, including creating a 'mini-Tate'.
  • Parents were enthusiastic and supportive of the project.

For Tate Liverpool

  • Staff had the opportunity to observe very young children accessing the collection over an extended period.
  • People working throughout the gallery, including security staff, became enthusiastic supporters of the project.
  • The project was endorsed at senior organisational level, with Tate investigating the possibility of future school residencies.
“Our vision is really about co-design, co-creation and co-production. So My Primary School is at the Museum is just a fantastic way of testing that out.” Lindsey Fryer, Tate Liverpool

What did they learn?

The project showed what could happen when very young children were given the time and freedom to explore a cultural environment rather than simply visiting it for a short, structured session.

For Tate staff, the extended residency provided a different way of observing children's engagement with the collection. For nursery practitioners, it offered the chance to experiment with new approaches and then bring ideas back into the nursery setting.

Challenges

Finding sufficient space for a two-week project had an impact on access for other groups using Tate Liverpool. Staff resources were also stretched, with additional adults needed to relieve some of the pressure of supervising activities.

Project details

  • Location: Tate Liverpool, Albert Dock
  • Date: 29 February – 11 March 2016
  • Children: 24 pre-school children, aged 3–4
  • Daily attendance: Maximum 15 children
  • Nursery: Kensington Children's Centre and Nursery

South Shields · 2016

My Primary School is at Arbeia Roman Fort

At Arbeia Roman Fort, directly opposite their school, a Year 5 class experienced familiar subjects in a stimulating new environment.

Over three months, the children discovered how a heritage site could support learning across a much broader curriculum than history alone. By the end of the project, they were confident and knowledgeable enough to act as tour guides for parents and visitors.

The project also changed how the school viewed the site. Hadrian Primary School began planning to make much greater use of Arbeia, while the museum wanted to develop its relationships with local schools and reach families who may never otherwise have visited.

The setting

Hadrian Primary School is situated close to the River Tyne, the coastline, South Marine Park and Arbeia Roman Fort. The school's location gives its pupils access to a rich geographical, cultural and historical environment.

Built around AD 150, Arbeia Roman Fort was a key garrison and military supply base and guarded the main sea route to Hadrian's Wall. The site includes excavated remains and full-scale reconstructions, including the West Gate, Commanding Officer's house and a soldier's barrack.

Learning across the curriculum

The project's connection with history was an obvious starting point, but class teacher Stephanie Christie quickly found opportunities to teach geography, maths and literacy at the site as well.

Writing became an important part of the children's work. They kept their own log books, recorded their experiences and were keen to tell the whole school and their families what they had been doing. They also used Twitter to share updates about the project.

Museum workshops provided further opportunities to explore Roman life. During a 'time quest' activity, the children became archaeologists, excavated Roman artefacts and recorded what they discovered in diary entries.

“The creativity in the writing was phenomenal.” Stephanie Christie, class teacher

Using the site differently

The children spent more time outside than they normally would, exploring the extensive archaeological remains. This encouraged their teacher to look at familiar subjects in new ways.

In maths, for example, the children went on an angle hunt using protractors and searched for Roman numerals around the site.

The museum team also found that the project opened up new possibilities for using the site across the curriculum.

“For teaching across a broad curriculum, we've definitely discovered lots of new ways to utilise the space.” Leslie Palanker-Jermyn, Arbeia

Becoming tour guides

One of the most memorable activities was a slavery workshop. The children experienced aspects of everyday life through practical activities including baking bread, making herbal remedies, bed making and washing dirty nappies, under the watchful eye of the commanding officer's wife, played by learning officer Leslie Palanker-Jermyn.

Towards the end of the project, the children drew on everything they had learned to interpret the heritage of Arbeia themselves. They acted as tour guides for parents and visitors, sharing their knowledge of the site with confidence.

“There is a change in their maturity, in how they approach the space and the building.” Leslie Palanker-Jermyn, Arbeia

What changed?

For the children

  • Increased enthusiasm for learning encouraged the children to generate their own ideas for teaching sessions.
  • Writing about their activities and using new vocabulary supported improvements in literacy.
  • The children developed a strong desire to share their experience and knowledge with other children and their families.
  • Their confidence and maturity grew as they took ownership of the site and their learning.
“They've got an even deeper interest in their learning than they had before.” Stephanie Christie, class teacher

For Hadrian Primary School

  • Staff discovered a valuable and easily accessible teaching resource on their doorstep.
  • The project encouraged a more creative and flexible approach to teaching.
  • The site was identified as having potential for staff training and meetings as well as pupil learning.
“I think it's opened their eyes to actually what we've got on our doorstep, which is a fantastic site that you can use for learning with the children.” Stephanie Christie, class teacher

For Arbeia Roman Fort

  • Hadrian Primary provided a useful model for building a strong relationship between a museum and a local school.
  • Museum staff developed new ideas for using the site across the whole curriculum.
  • The project provided an opportunity to work through the practical and logistical challenges of accommodating school groups.
  • The experience helped the museum consider how to develop stronger relationships with local schools and families.

Challenges

Not every part of the curriculum transferred easily to the fort. Subjects such as PE and ICT were difficult to incorporate into the setting.

The museum also had to make practical adaptations to the site, including changes to the acoustics in one teaching space. Coordinating school routines such as registration, break times and assemblies could also make it difficult to use time as effectively as possible.

Project details

  • Location: Arbeia Roman Fort, South Shields
  • Date: 4 January – 23 March 2016
  • Children: 29 Year 5 pupils, aged 9–10
  • School: Hadrian Primary School
  • Museum: Arbeia Roman Fort & Museum

Cambridge · 2018–19

Nursery in Residence

What happens when young children are given the time and attention to explore museum and garden spaces on their own terms?

The University of Cambridge Museums' Nursery in Residence project brought together nine young children, nursery practitioners and museum educators for five days of observation, exploration and reflection.

The project involved educators from Cambridge University Botanic Garden and the Fitzwilliam Museum, with the team documenting what they saw through field notes, photographs, practitioner surveys and video.

A shared process of learning

The project was built around a cycle of observation, reflection, planning and action. Rather than starting with a fixed programme of activities, the educators paid close attention to what interested the children and used those observations to shape what happened next.

The approach was influenced by the work of Loris Malaguzzi, who wrote about the importance of standing aside, making room for learning and observing children carefully before deciding how to teach.

“Stand aside for a while and leave room for learning, observe what children do, and then, if you have understood it well, perhaps teaching will be different from before.” Loris Malaguzzi

Young children care about our collections

One of the project's key findings was that young children were deeply engaged with the collections and spaces around them.

Objects became ways of connecting different experiences, while the children used a mixture of play, conversation, movement and making meaning together.

The project also exposed a tension between freedom and control. Giving children genuine freedom to explore required adults to reconsider how much they directed, and how much they allowed to unfold.

Learning to listen

The experience had an impact beyond the five days themselves. Nursery practitioners described how the project changed the way they listened to and observed children back in the nursery.

“I tried even more when I came back. I thought, these children had this gift that we gave them: all this attention. So I tried to do it even more when I came back.” Post-project interview with a nursery practitioner

The practitioner described the experience as giving children something they particularly needed: the feeling of being listened to.

A sense of belonging

The project also suggested that spending sustained time in cultural spaces could help young children develop a genuine sense of ownership and belonging.

“It was just so beautiful to think: this is our place; we know this place. That's what you want: for children to feel free. That they belong there.” Post-project interview with a nursery practitioner

In one recorded exchange, an adult asked:

“Whose garden is this then?” Transcription from video footage

The child's answer was simple:

“Ours - children's.” Alanna, recorded during the project

Educators as learners

The project was not simply about what the children learned. The educators were learners too.

By observing the children closely and reflecting together, nursery and museum staff gained new perspectives on their own practice and on the ways children engage with cultural spaces.

“There is a constant relational reciprocity between those who educate and those who are educated, between those who teach and those who learn.” Carlina Rinaldi, 2001

What did they discover?

  • Young children can engage meaningfully with museum and garden collections.
  • Objects can help children connect different experiences and ideas.
  • Careful observation can reveal interests and learning that adults might otherwise overlook.
  • Giving children more freedom requires adults to think differently about their own role.
  • The experience influenced nursery practitioners' practice beyond the project itself.
  • Spending sustained time in cultural spaces can help children develop a sense that those spaces belong to them.

What happened next?

The project led to a new line of enquiry. Having seen how much the nine participating children gained from the experience, the nursery team wanted to explore how similar opportunities could be extended to all the children.

“We could see how much those nine children got from it, so actually, let's branch it out, let's give this kind of experience to all the children.” Post-project interview with a nursery practitioner

Project details

  • Location: University of Cambridge Museums
  • Duration: 5 days
  • Children: 9
  • Educators: 7
  • Research material: 15 practitioner surveys, 25 pages of field notes and around 500 photographs
